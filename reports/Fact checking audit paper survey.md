# Build a CPU-friendly, auditable fact-checker: the paper map

The strongest project design for a final-year, CPU-only student is a **decompose, retrieve, verify, justify** fact-checker. It uses a small encoder verifier (MiniCheck or AlignScore class) with BM25 plus a small embedding model for retrieval. A hosted or quantised LLM is called only for low-confidence claims. Wrap it in an audit layer that no existing paper provides in integrated form. The foundations are RAG ([Lewis et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11401)), the FEVER and AVeriTeC benchmarks, atomic-claim decomposition (FActScore, ProgramFC, Loki), and the ALCE/AlignScore/MiniCheck line for citation checking. The audit layer rests on Guo et al. calibration, conformal prediction, CheckList/TextAttack robustness testing, fairlearn disaggregated metrics, and drift work on temporal generalisation. Realistic numbers are modest. AVeriTeC's best 2024 score was **0.63** with proprietary GPT-4o, but the 2025 open-weights, single-GPU edition was won at only **0.33** ([ACL 2025.fever-1.15](https://aclanthology.org/2025.fever-1.15/)). A CPU project should aim for the low-to-mid range and win on audit depth, not headline accuracy. Under the EU AI Act, a general fact-checker is most likely not high-risk. The binding duties are Art. 50 transparency (from 2 Aug 2026) and Art. 4 AI literacy, and Arts. 9-15 serve as a voluntary yardstick. The research gap is clear: the notes found **no integrated audit of calibration, per-slice fairness, robustness and drift for a claim-verification pipeline**. Several items below are search-only or unconfirmed, and they are flagged where they appear.

## Fact-checking engine: from RAG foundations to AVeriTeC systems

The pattern that recurs across the literature is to split a claim or answer into atomic claims, generate queries, retrieve with BM25 and/or dense retrieval, verify each claim against its evidence, then aggregate a verdict with a justification and evidence pointers. Iterative, confidence-gated retrieval (FIRE) cuts cost without losing accuracy, and the best AVeriTeC systems retrieve evidence as question-answer pairs. The tables list each paper with its link, a one-line summary and why it matters.

## Citation faithfulness and CPU-scale verifiers

Attribution is measured as whether the cited passage entails the statement, via human AIS annotation or NLI and alignment models as proxies. Recent work argues that entailment-based correctness is not the same as causal faithfulness.

| Paper | One-line summary | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| [AIS, Rashkin et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.12870) | Defines "Attributable to Identified Sources" with a human annotation protocol. | The gold standard automatic metrics approximate. |
| [Attributed QA, Bohnet et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.08037) | Reproducible evaluation for attributed LLMs. | Metric-human correlation methodology. |
| [ALCE, Gao et al., EMNLP 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14627) | First automatic citation benchmark (fluency, correctness, citation recall/precision); even the best models lack full citation support 50% of the time on ELI5. | Use its metrics for your faithfulness axis. |
| [TRUE, Honovich et al., NAACL 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.04991) | Meta-evaluation of factual-consistency metrics over 11 datasets. | Justifies using NLI-class checkers. |
| [AlignScore, Zha et al., ACL 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.16739) | 355M-parameter alignment model, evaluated on 22 datasets, competitive with GPT-4-class judges. | RoBERTa-large size, so CPU-feasible. |
| [MiniCheck, Tang et al., EMNLP 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.10774) ([model card](https://huggingface.co/lytang/MiniCheck-Flan-T5-Large)) | 770M Flan-T5 verifier claimed GPT-4-level at about 400x lower cost; releases LLM-AggreFact (11 datasets, about 29K examples); MIT licence. | Best-documented small verifier. The model card requires CUDA for the official package. |
| [Wallat et al., "Correctness is not Faithfulness," SIGIR 2025](https://staff.fnwi.uva.nl/m.derijke/wp-content/papercite-data/pdf/wallat-2025-correctness.pdf) | Separates citation correctness from causal faithfulness; reports up to 57% post-rationalised citations. | Core framing for the audit. The 57% figure comes from a secondary search summary, so check the paper [unverified]. |
| [Attribution, Citation and Quotation survey, 2025 (arXiv 2508.15396)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.15396) | Survey of evidence-based generation. | Listed in search, not read [search-only]. |
| ["Verify with Caution" (arXiv 2501.14883)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.14883) | Pitfalls of imperfect factuality metrics. | A warning about verifier reliability. Not read [search-only]. |

MiniCheck reports balanced accuracy on LLM-AggreFact of **74.7%** (MiniCheck-FT5) against **75.3%** for GPT-4, **74.1%** for Claude-3 Opus and **70.4%** for AlignScore; the model card quotes 75.0 for Flan-T5-L. These numbers come from a search summary of the [leaderboard](https://llm-aggrefact.github.io/), not the full paper table, so confirm them yourself. The full MiniCheck PDF came back as binary and could not be parsed.

For retrieval and compact models, the supporting papers are [DPR](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.04906) (beats Lucene-BM25 by 9-19% absolute top-20 accuracy on open-domain QA), [BEIR](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08663) (BM25 is a robust zero-shot baseline, which supports a hybrid design), [Sentence-BERT](https://arxiv.org/abs/1908.10084), [MiniLM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.10957), [DeBERTaV3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2111.09543), and [BGE/C-Pack](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.07597) (parameter counts not listed in the fetched summary). For quantisation, [LLM.int8()](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.07339) is GPU-oriented. For CPU, the notes suggest ONNX Runtime or GGUF/llama.cpp, but that was **not verified**.

## CPU-only laptop: what is realistic

The notes' suggested stack is a design proposal, not a tested result. Use BM25 (rank_bm25 or Lucene) plus bge-small or all-MiniLM for hybrid retrieval, a small cross-encoder reranker, then an AlignScore- or MiniCheck-class verifier exported to ONNX/int8. Call an LLM (hosted API or heavily quantised local model) only for justification text or low-confidence claims, FIRE-style. Expect lower accuracy than LLM systems on multi-hop claims, because the AVeriTeC winners used GPT-4o or 70B-class open models. Avoid Wikipedia-scale indexing: skip full FEVER and FEVEROUS, or use only the shipped sentence subsets. The sensible dataset plan is AVeriTeC dev (500 claims) for headline evaluation, Climate-FEVER and SciFact for domain shift, and LIAR for subgroup and calibration audits, which needs no retrieval. Key unknowns the student must measure: CPU latency and memory for AlignScore, MiniCheck and DeBERTa-NLI; ONNX/int8 accuracy loss; the AVeriTeC knowledge-store download size and embedding time; and bge-small/MiniLM BEIR scores. None of these were found in the sources.

## Audit and trustworthy-AI methods: calibration, robustness, fairness, drift

**Calibration.** Binned ECE with reliability diagrams and temperature scaling is the de facto standard ([Guo et al., ICML 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.04599)). [Desai & Durrett, EMNLP 2020](https://aclanthology.org/2020.emnlp-main.21/) show BERT/RoBERTa are calibrated in-domain out of the box, with out-of-domain error up to 3.5x lower than baselines. [Minderer et al., NeurIPS 2021](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/2021/file/8420d359404024567b5aefda1231af24-Paper.pdf) is image-domain, so transfer to NLP is an inference. ECE is criticised: [Nixon et al.](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content_CVPRW_2019/papers/Uncertainty%20and%20Robustness%20in%20Deep%20Visual%20Learning/Nixon_Measuring_Calibration_in_Deep_Learning_CVPRW_2019_paper.pdf) show bin choice changes the value and propose SCE and ACE, and ECE is not a proper score ([arXiv 2203.07835](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.07835); the zero-ECE-for-a-constant-predictor claim comes from a [secondary summary](https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/expected-calibration-error)). The survey [arXiv 2308.01222](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2308.01222) and the recent critique [arXiv 2605.01796](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.01796) are [search-only]. For LLMs, [Kadavath et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2207.05221) show P(True) self-evaluation works but degrades out-of-distribution. [Tian et al., EMNLP 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14975) report verbalised confidence from RLHF models beats raw token probabilities (ECE reduction above 50% on some QA sets). That ID and the figure came from a secondary tweet snippet, so verify on arXiv. Several 2025-26 verbalised-confidence papers surfaced in search but were not read [search-only]: [2605.27752](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.27752), [2609.26489](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.26489v1), [2509.25532](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.25532), [2406.04370](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.04370). The practical recommendation is to report ECE alongside Brier/NLL, a reliability diagram, adaptive or equal-mass bins, and class-conditional ECE. Top-label ECE hides class-wise miscalibration in three-way SUPPORTS/REFUTES/NEI verdicts. The debiased-ECE literature (Kumar, Widmann, Gupta) is known to exist but was not checked [unverified].

**Conformal prediction.** [Campos et al., TACL 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.01976) is the NLP survey (distribution-free coverage guarantees). [Quach et al., ICLR 2024](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.10193) calibrates sampling stop rules. For black-box APIs, "API Is Enough" ([arXiv 2403.01216](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01216v1), author list unverified), [Conformal Alignment (2405.10301)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2405.10301) and [Domain-Shift-Aware CP (2510.05566)](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.05566v1) are all [search-only]. A verdict set that contains the truth with at least 1-alpha probability is an auditable guarantee that does not depend on ECE. Coverage is marginal, so it must be checked per subgroup. The notes found no application of conformal prediction to claim verification specifically.

**Robustness.** [CheckList, Ribeiro et al., ACL 2020](https://aclanthology.org/2020.acl-main.442/) supplies MFT, INV and DIR tests, and practitioners found about 3x more bugs. [TextAttack](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.05909) (16 attacks, pip package), [TextFlint](https://aclanthology.org/2021.acl-demo.41/) (20 general plus 60 task-specific transformations), [Polyjuice](https://aclanthology.org/2021.acl-long.523/) for counterfactuals, and [PromptBench](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.04528) (word-level attacks cost 39% on average) cover perturbation testing. Fact-checking-specific work includes the [FEVER2.0 shared task](https://aclanthology.org/D19-6601/) (1,174 breaker claims), [Thorne et al. adversarial attacks](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1903.05543) (authors and venue unverified beyond the snippet), the [EMNLP 2025 survey of adversarial attacks on automated fact-checking](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.08463), and [Schuster et al., EMNLP 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1908.05267). That last paper shows claim-only BERT reaches 61.7% on FEVER, and the top shared-task system drops from 81.8% to 58.7% on the Symmetric test set. The follow-up [Counterfactual Debiasing, ACL 2023](https://aclanthology.org/2023.acl-long.374.pdf) is [search-only]. Retrieval-stage attacks (poisoned evidence, prompt injection) are a natural extension tied to AI Act Art. 15(5).

**Fairness.** Without obvious protected attributes, the literature uses topic, political connotation, language or region, publisher and time as the groups. The reference texts are [Barocas, Hardt & Narayanan](https://fairmlbook.org/) for the formal criteria and [Fairlearn (arXiv 2303.16626)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2303.16626) with [MetricFrame](https://fairlearn.org/v0.14/api_reference/generated/fairlearn.metrics.MetricFrame.html) for disaggregated metrics. Fact-checking-specific evidence includes:

| Paper | One-line summary | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| [PolBiX, Jakob et al., Findings EMNLP 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.15335) | Minimal pairs of German claims differing only in political connotation; judgmental words shift truthfulness assessments across six LLMs even when prompted to be objective. | Best available counterfactual-invariance definition; German-only. |
| [Cross-language unfairness, IP&M 2026](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0306457326000087) | Political bias explains the most unfairness for English and Portuguese claims. | [search-only]: the page returned 403, authors and abstract not retrieved. |
| [Proma et al., "How LLMs Fail to Support Fact-Checking"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.01902) | ChatGPT, Gemini and Claude struggle to ground responses in news sources and tend to cite left-leaning sources. | Retrieval and source-selection fairness. |
| [Bakke & Heggelund, "(Fact) Check Your Bias"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.21745) | With HerO on Llama 3.1, about half of claims came out NEI on parametric knowledge; verdicts stayed fairly stable under perspective-injected evidence. | Audits a real pipeline's perspective sensitivity. |
| [Coelho et al., geographic inequality](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.22877) | Global North statements verified better than Global South across six regions (600 statements). | Geographic subgroup template. |
| [Verhoeven et al., "Yesterday's News"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.18122) | Benchmarks generalisation across time, event, topic, publisher and political bias. | Effectively a slice framework for misinformation. |
| ["Political Bias Audits ... Sycophancy" (arXiv 2604.27633)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.27633) | Audit outcomes depend on who the model thinks is asking. | [search-only]. Warns against single-prompt audits. |
| [Independent fact-checkers' political neutrality (arXiv 2407.19498)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.19498) | Questions neutrality of the source labels. | Abstract not read in detail. All real-world labels come from fact-checkers. |

The notes' own synthesis, which no source formally defines: use metadata slices as pseudo-protected groups. Require bounded gaps in accuracy, macro-F1 and per-slice ECE; verdict invariance on minimal pairs (PolBiX/CheckList INV); and separation-style parity (equal false-positive and false-negative rates of "false" labels). Base rates of falsehood legitimately differ by topic, so demographic parity (independence) is inappropriate. Slice labels will often be LLM-inferred and noisy, and fairlearn only handles supplied attributes. Fairlearn's confidence intervals for many small intersectional slices were not verified.

**Drift and temporal generalisation.** [Lazaridou et al., "Mind the Gap," NeurIPS 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.01951) shows stale models degrade (up to 16% perplexity difference per a search summary). Other items are [search-only]: [temporal concept drift and explanations](https://aclanthology.org/2022.findings-emnlp.298.pdf), [Evidence-Based Temporal Fact Verification](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.15291v2), [Implicit Temporal Reasoning, Findings EACL 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.12569), [TSVer, EMNLP 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.emnlp-main.1519.pdf) (reported gains of up to 9% micro-F1, unverified), [Forecasting Temporal Trends](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.14728), and [Wild-Time](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.14238). The time-aware fact-checking papers study modelling, not audit monitoring. Follow-ups on factual-knowledge staleness (TemporalWiki and similar) were not checked.

**Dataset artifacts and leakage.** [Glockner et al., EMNLP 2022](https://aclanthology.org/2022.emnlp-main.397/) show existing datasets leak evidence from fact-checking articles. AVeriTeC mitigates this with pre-claim evidence and chronological splits, but organisers found date-formatting errors let post-claim articles into the knowledge store, and the scraper failed on 297 of 500 dev examples ([shared-task paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.23850)). Label noise is documented for SciFact: [Sylvestre, BioNLP 2026](https://aclanthology.org/2026.bionlp-1.9/) found 5.3% errors in 209 audited dev pairs, and fixing them raises binary macro-F1 by 1.7-3.8 points. [Pelrine et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14928) release LIAR-New with context-sufficiency labels. LIAR metadata also leaks credit-history counts ([secondary](https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/liar-benchmark)). Topic imbalances such as police-related items skewing false in Snopes come from search snippets of [arXiv 2407.02122](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.02122v2) and [2108.12601](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2108.12601), and must be verified before quoting.

**Tooling.** [netcal](https://github.com/EFS-OpenSource/calibration-framework) (ECE, ACE, MCE, temperature scaling), [MAPIE](https://github.com/scikit-learn-contrib/MAPIE) ([paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.12274)) for conformal sets, fairlearn, TextAttack, TextFlint, CheckList and PromptBench. scikit-learn provides `brier_score_loss`, `log_loss` and `calibration_curve`; this comes from background knowledge and was not source-checked.

## LLM-as-judge and RAG evaluation: validate before you trust

An audit layer that scores faithfulness with an LLM judge must itself be audited. The foundational result is [Zheng et al., NeurIPS 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05685): a GPT-4 judge reaches over 80% agreement with human preferences but shows position, verbosity and self-enhancement bias. [Chiang & Lee](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.01937) and [G-Eval](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.16634) (Spearman 0.514 on summarisation) support judge use. Bias studies include [position bias](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.17926), [Shi et al. (15 judges, about 150,000 instances)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.07791), [length control](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.04475) (Arena correlation 0.94 to 0.98), [self-preference](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.13076), [perplexity-linked bias](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.21819), and the [12-bias CALM taxonomy](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.02736). Reliability studies are the most sobering. [Thakur et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.12624) find only the largest models align reasonably with humans. [JUDGE-BENCH](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.18403) finds wide variability by property. [Szymanski et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.20266) report experts agreed with LLM judges only 68% (dietetics) and 64% (mental health). [Clarke & Dietz](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.17156) argue LLM judgments cannot replace humans in IR. The survey is [Gu et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.15594).

The validation protocol proposed in the notes is an inference, not a published standard: human-label a stratified sample of 100-300 claims with two annotators; report inter-annotator kappa or Krippendorff alpha; report judge-vs-human kappa (not raw agreement) with a confusion matrix; run swap-order and length-controlled tests; use a judge from a different model family than the generator; fix temperature at 0 with repeated runs; re-validate on any version change; and bootstrap confidence intervals. No source gave an acceptance threshold for kappa, and the Landis-Koch bands are conventional but unsourced.

For RAG evaluation, [RAGAS](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.15217) (faithfulness, context precision, context recall; [docs](https://docs.ragas.io/en/stable/concepts/metrics/available_metrics/)), [ARES](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.09476) (small fine-tuned judges plus prediction-powered inference with a few hundred human labels), and [RAGChecker](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.08067) (claim-level diagnostics) are the main frameworks. Most RAGAS metrics need LLM calls, so cost scales with samples times metrics. The EACL 2024 venue for RAGAS is from background knowledge [unverified]. The cheapest viable setup is a non-LLM NLI check (AlignScore/MiniCheck) as the primary faithfulness metric, with RAGAS or a small judge on a sampled subset. DeepEval, TruLens, TRACe/RAGBench, HHEM, Prometheus, JudgeLM, prompt-injection attacks on judges, and LLM-as-judge for fact-checking specifically were not researched.

## Documentation frameworks and the EU AI Act

**Documentation.** [Model Cards, Mitchell et al., FAT* 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.03993) report performance across groups and conditions. [Datasheets for Datasets, Gebru et al., CACM 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.09010) document data provenance and labelling. [NIST AI RMF 1.0](https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework) (26 Jan 2023; Govern, Map, Measure, Manage) and its Generative AI Profile NIST AI 600-1 (26 Jul 2024) provide the risk structure. Use a model card for the verdict pipeline and the judge, a datasheet for your evaluation set, and the Measure function for judge validation. These double as evidence for Art. 11/13/Annex XII. Subcategory IDs and the AI 600-1 risk list were not verified, and no field-by-field model card to Annex IV comparison was found.

**EU AI Act status as of 6 Oct 2026.** The Act is [Regulation (EU) 2024/1689](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj/eng). The Digital Omnibus on AI, [Regulation (EU) 2026/1744](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2026/1744/oj/eng), was adopted 8 July 2026, published 24 July and in force 27 July 2026 ([summary](https://lawandtechnology.eu/en/digital-omnibus-on-ai-official-journal-regulation-2026-1744/); [EP Legislative Train](https://www.europarl.europa.eu/legislative-train/package-digital-package/file-digital-omnibus-on-ai)). **Caveat: the researchers could not open the EUR-Lex pages; article text came from the [artificialintelligenceact.eu](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/) mirror and law-firm summaries. Quote final wording from EUR-Lex.** The key dates ([Art. 113](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/113/); [Gibson Dunn](https://www.gibsondunn.com/eu-ai-act-omnibus-agreement-postponed-high-risk-deadlines-and-other-key-changes/); [Morgan Lewis](https://www.morganlewis.com/pubs/2026/06/eu-approves-delays-and-other-amendments-to-certain-eu-ai-act-obligations-what-businesses-should-know)) are:

| Provision | Applies from |
|---|---|
| Chapters I-II (Art. 4 AI literacy, Art. 5 prohibitions) | 2 Feb 2025 |
| GPAI obligations (Art. 53), governance, penalties | 2 Aug 2025 |
| Art. 50 transparency | 2 Aug 2026 |
| Art. 50(2) machine-readable marking grace period (pre-existing systems; sources differ in wording, so check the text of 2026/1744) | to 2 Dec 2026 |
| High-risk Annex III systems (moved from 2 Aug 2026) | 2 Dec 2027 |
| High-risk Annex I systems (moved from 2 Aug 2027) | 2 Aug 2028 |

A general fact-checking or misinformation-flagging tool is most likely **not high-risk**, since it is not an Annex III use case unless deployed in listed areas such as judicial assistance or influencing voting behaviour ([Annex III](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/annex/3/)). That is the researchers' inference, not legal advice, and no source answered the classification question directly. A documented Art. 6(3)-(4) classification memo is advisable ([Art. 6](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/6/)).

What binds a likely non-high-risk system:

- **Art. 50(1)** requires that users are told they are interacting with AI. **Art. 50(2)** requires machine-readable marking of synthetic text outputs. **Art. 50(4)** requires deployers to disclose AI-generated text published on matters of public interest, unless it underwent human editorial review ([Art. 50](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/50/)). The Commission published a voluntary final Code of Practice on marking and labelling on 10 Jun 2026, recommending at least two layers of marking ([Jones Day](https://www.jonesday.com/en/insights/2026/06/european-commission-publishes-final-code-of-practice-on-marking-and-labelling-aigenerated-content)). Art. 50 was not textually amended by the omnibus ([regulation-ai.eu](https://www.regulation-ai.eu/en/changes/)). Status of the Commission's Art. 50 guidelines is unknown.
- **Art. 4 AI literacy** was softened to "take measures to support" literacy but still applies.
- **Art. 53** applies to GPAI model providers ([Art. 53](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/53/)). A project that only deploys a third-party LLM is a downstream user and should request the Annex XII information.
- **Fines** under [Art. 99](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/99/) reach EUR 15M or 3% for operator-obligation breaches including transparency, with lower caps for SMEs.

Arts. 9-15 are the best-practice yardstick, binding only if the system is high-risk: [Art. 12](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/12/) (event logging), [Art. 13](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/13/) (instructions with accuracy metrics, known failure conditions, performance on specific groups) and [Art. 15](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/15/) (accuracy, robustness, feedback-loop avoidance, resilience to poisoning and adversarial inputs). Arts. 9, 10, 11, 14, 72, 73, 101 and Annex IV were not fetched. The suggested audit-report mapping is: 50(1) to a UI disclosure; 50(2) to metadata marking of generated verdicts; 13/15(3) to published accuracy metrics and failure conditions; 12 to logs of claims, evidence, judge scores and model versions; 15(4) to not retraining on your own judge outputs; 15(5) to prompt-injection and evidence-poisoning tests. Say "voluntary alignment with Arts. 9-15", not "compliant". Conflicting sources: [regulation-ai.eu](https://www.regulation-ai.eu/en/changes/) says Art. 5 was unchanged while the law firms report a new prohibition; this is irrelevant to fact-checking.

## Research gaps the project can fill

The notes' searches (about 20 calls on the audit side) found no integrated, continuous audit layer for a deployed claim-verification pipeline. Absence of a hit is not proof of absence, so the student should run a short targeted literature check on each item before claiming novelty. The candidate gaps, in rough order of tractability on a laptop:

1. **Per-slice calibration and conformal coverage for claim verification.** No work was found on calibration or conformal coverage for RAG fact-checkers by topic, party or region. Dataset papers do not report ECE or subgroup breakdowns, and no ECE numbers exist for AVeriTeC or FEVER pipelines. LIAR and AVeriTeC metadata support this directly.
2. **A joint audit scorecard** covering per-slice calibration, conformal coverage, perturbation invariance, citation faithfulness and drift in one reproducible tool. Existing audits are single-axis (FEVER2.0 for robustness, FACT-AUDIT ([arXiv 2502.17924](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.17924)) for dynamic LLM evaluation, PolBiX for political bias).
3. **Operational fairness for non-protected slices.** No source formally defines it. A defensible contribution is the separation-plus-calibration-within-groups definition above, with uncertainty on inferred slice labels.
4. **Pipeline-level fairness including retrieval and source selection.** Only partly covered by Proma et al. and Bakke & Heggelund.
5. **Drift monitoring tied to claim date and event.** Time-aware fact-checking papers study modelling, not monitoring. Add a memorisation probe comparing pre-cutoff and post-cutoff claims, linked to the leakage work of Lu & Setiawan.
6. **Faithful rather than merely correct citations.** Generating answers from retrieved evidence only removes post-rationalisation by construction. Entailment alone cannot show the model used the citation, so state that limitation.
7. **Validated cheap judges.** Apply the judge-validation protocol to a CPU-scale verifier plus a small LLM judge, reporting kappa and bias probes. This directly serves AI Act Art. 13/15 documentation.
8. **Dataset-hygiene checks as part of the audit:** claim-only baselines on every dataset, evidence-date filtering, Symmetric or minimal-pair tests, and noise-aware reporting (treat gaps under 5 points with caution).

## Suggested reading order

Start with orientation: RAG (Lewis), then the AVeriTeC paper and the 2024 and 2025 shared-task reports (they define the task, scores and efficiency constraint). Read FActScore and Loki next for decomposition and an open pipeline, then FIRE and HerO 2 for the efficiency story. Then read the attribution cluster: ALCE, AlignScore, MiniCheck and "Correctness is not Faithfulness". Move to the audit foundations: Guo et al., Nixon et al. (ECE flaws), the Campos et al. conformal survey, CheckList, Schuster et al. and the fairlearn MetricFrame docs. Then read the fairness and bias papers (PolBiX, Coelho et al., Proma et al., Bakke & Heggelund, Verhoeven et al.) and the leakage and label-noise papers (Glockner et al., Lu & Setiawan, Sylvestre). Next cover judge reliability (Zheng et al., Thakur et al., JUDGE-BENCH) and RAGAS/ARES. Finish with governance: Model Cards, Datasheets, NIST AI RMF, then AI Act Arts. 50, 15, 13 and 12 on EUR-Lex. Save the [search-only] items for the final literature-check pass.

## Conclusion

The central finding is asymmetry. The engine side has mature, open, laptop-sized building blocks, but the audit side offers only single-axis studies, with the integrating work left to the student. That makes the audit layer, not the verdict accuracy, the defensible novelty. Treat a modest AVeriTeC score as acceptable if the audit shows why and where the system fails. The strongest differentiators are the ones the notes could not find anywhere: per-slice calibration and conformal coverage, drift tied to claim date, and faithfulness that is causal rather than merely entailed.

Two cautions carry the most risk. First, many benchmark gaps of a few points are within label noise (SciFact gold errors of 2-5%, cross-benchmark margins under 5 points), so report confidence intervals. Second, the legal framing changed this summer: the Omnibus moved the high-risk dates to 2027-2028 while Art. 50 transparency began on 2 Aug 2026. A tool that publishes AI-generated verdicts on public-interest matters should be built with disclosure and marking from day one, while describing Arts. 9-15 alignment as voluntary.
